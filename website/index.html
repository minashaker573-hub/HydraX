The field problem

Most irrigation systems know when to run. They don't know why.

A fixed schedule cannot see rainfall, a heatwave, a blocked line or a probe that has failed — it applies the same water on the same day regardless of what the ground actually needs. Walking zones to judge moisture by hand is slow and inconsistent between people. And a valve stuck open, or a pump left running, can waste water for hours before anyone notices — usually by seeing the result, not the cause.

Many connected controllers make this worse: they send a reading up and wait for an instruction back. On a farm with intermittent connectivity, that means the thing deciding whether crops get water is somewhere else entirely.

How HYDRAX works

Soil signals, a local decision, water where it's needed.

Every step below runs on the controller itself. The backend and dashboard only observe it — see the platform for what that looks like.

  1. 1

    Soil signals

    Two capacitive probes per zone read moisture continuously. Each reading is filtered against spikes and checked for a plausible electrical range before anything trusts it.

    median-filtered · range-checked · per probe

  2. 2

    A local decision

    The controller compares each zone's reading against its own start and stop thresholds and decides — on the device, without waiting on a network round trip to answer.

    hysteresis band · minimum runtime · cooldown

  3. 3

    Healthier crops

    Water reaches a zone because its own soil called for it — not because a calendar said so. That's the aim the whole loop above is built around.

The same decision, made the same way, every time a zone reads dry.

Benefits

What the system actually does, stated plainly.

Every line below is implemented and covered by the firmware's automated test suite — a fact about the code, not a projection about a farm.

Local-first

The irrigation controller holds no reference to Wi-Fi or HTTP. A host test runs a complete irrigation cycle with no network object present at all, at any point in the process.

Zone-based

Each zone holds its own thresholds and its own state. When more than one reads dry, the driest is served first — one at a time, because there is one pump.

Hysteresis

Starting and stopping use two different thresholds, not one setpoint, so a reading sitting at the boundary doesn't switch the pump on and off repeatedly.

Runtime protection

A maximum runtime cuts the pump if a run goes long, and locks that zone out until it has been reviewed — a bounded worst case, not an assumption of a happy path.

Offline operation

None of the above depends on a network connection reaching the farm at all — the controller decides alone, and reports what it did once the link is back.

Built for the field

Designed for real conditions, not a demo bench.

The engineering exists to be checked, not taken on faith — including the parts still in progress. The controller compiles for its target hardware and is verified against 50 firmware tests; running on a physical board, and calibrating each probe against real soil, is the next step, not a claimed one.
