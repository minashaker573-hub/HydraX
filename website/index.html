Edge-controlled irrigation

The decision to water happens in the field.

Built for small and medium farms, HYDRAX measures soil moisture in each zone and decides locally whether to irrigate. The controller owns that decision. The network is for watching — never for deciding.

The problem

Most irrigation runs on a timer, not on the soil.

A schedule cannot see rainfall, a heatwave, a blocked line or a probe that has failed. It applies the same water on the same day regardless of what the ground actually needs.

Watering by clock

Fixed schedules apply water the soil may not need, and withhold it when a hot week means it does. The controller has no idea which is happening.

Checking by hand

Walking zones to judge moisture is slow, easy to postpone, and inconsistent between people. Small farms end up guessing.

Faults run unnoticed

A valve that fails open or a pump left running can waste water for hours before anyone notices — usually by seeing the result, not the cause.

Cloud-dependent controllers stop deciding

Many connected systems send readings up and wait for an answer. On a farm with intermittent connectivity that is a design flaw, not an edge case: when the link drops, the thing that decides whether crops get water is somewhere else. HYDRAX treats the network as optional by construction.

How it works

One loop, running on the controller.

Every step below happens on the ESP32. The backend and dashboard observe the loop; they are never inside it.

  1. 01

    Sense

    Two capacitive probes per zone. Each reading is median-filtered against spikes, smoothed, and checked against a plausible electrical range — a probe reading near the rail is treated as a fault, not as very dry soil.

  2. 02

    Understand

    A zone's moisture is the mean of the probes that are actually working. A failed probe is excluded rather than averaged in as zero, so one dead sensor cannot drag a zone's reading down and trigger watering it does not need.

  3. 03

    Decide

    Irrigation starts below a start threshold and stops at a higher stop threshold. The gap between them is deliberate: a single setpoint makes a pump switch on and off repeatedly around the boundary. Starting also requires both probes in a zone to be healthy.

  4. 04

    Act

    The zone valve opens first and the pump follows, never the reverse. One zone runs at a time. A maximum runtime cuts the pump if a run goes long, and the zone is locked out until an operator has looked at it.

  5. 05

    Monitor

    Readings and events are published to the dashboard. If the link is down they are buffered on the device and sent when it returns — while irrigation carries on regardless.

Features

What the system actually does today.

Everything listed here is implemented and covered by the automated test suite. Planned work is kept separate, further down.

Local-first control

The irrigation controller holds no reference to Wi-Fi or HTTP. A test runs a full irrigation cycle with no network object present at all.

Hysteresis, not a setpoint

Configurable start and stop thresholds per zone, with a minimum run time and a per-zone cooldown as independent backstops against short-cycling.

Independent zones

Each zone has its own thresholds and its own state. When several are dry, the driest is served first — one at a time, because there is one pump.

Interlocks in firmware

The pump cannot start with every valve closed. Two valves cannot open together. Closing the active valve cuts the pump first. These hold even if the logic above them is wrong.

Fails safe, by default

Outputs are driven off before they are configured as outputs, so a reset mid-run cannot leave a pump latched on. Losing sensor data stops irrigation rather than guessing.

Degraded, not dead

A zone that loses one probe keeps an existing run going on the survivor but cannot start a new one. Losing both stops that zone without taking the farm down.

Survives the network

Telemetry is buffered while offline and flushed on reconnect. Reconnection uses capped exponential backoff and never blocks the control loop.

Monitoring dashboard

Seven sections covering soil, irrigation, pump, water network, safety, alerts and device health — with real moisture history, not a decorative chart.

Says what it cannot measure

Any reading with no sensor behind it displays NOT AVAILABLE rather than a zero or an estimate. See why that matters.

Differentiation

How this compares to what most farms already have.

Every line below is a property of the control logic, not a comparison against a named competing product — the same behaviour is what the firmware test suite in Technology actually checks.

Versus manual irrigation

A person has to notice a zone is dry and act on it, often after the moment has passed. HYDRAX checks every zone continuously and starts irrigating the instant its threshold is crossed, with nobody needing to be on-site.

Versus a timer controller

A timer runs on a schedule regardless of what the soil needs, so it wastes water on a wet day and under-waters a hot one. HYDRAX's decision is a soil reading, not a clock.

Versus single-setpoint moisture automation

A single threshold makes a pump switch on and off repeatedly right at the boundary. HYDRAX uses a start/stop hysteresis band, a minimum run time and a per-zone cooldown — three independent backstops, not one.

Versus cloud-first IoT platforms

Most connected controllers send readings up and wait for an instruction back — a design that stops deciding the moment the connection drops. HYDRAX's control core contains no networking code at all; the cloud is optional and exists for visibility, never for the decision.

A design decision worth stating

An instrument that guesses is worse than one that admits it does not know.

HYDRAX has no flow meter, no pump current sensor and no leak detection. A dashboard could easily infer litres from pump runtime and print a confident number. We do not, because that number would be wrong exactly when it matters most — a blocked line and a burst pipe both run the pump while moving very different volumes of water.

So those panels read NOT AVAILABLE, with the reason stated. The user interface is already built for the sensors; it holds no data until the sensors exist.

The same rule governs this website. Nothing here is a projection, a simulation presented as a result, or a figure we have not measured.

Technology

Built to be inspected.

The control logic is separated from the hardware it runs on, which is what makes it testable without a board — and auditable by someone who did not write it.

Controller

  • ESP32, C++17 on the Arduino framework
  • Pure control core with no hardware headers
  • Sensors, actuators, radio and clock behind interfaces
  • Networking on a separate core, so it cannot stall control
  • Explicit state machine — no hidden state

Backend

  • Node.js and TypeScript, no runtime dependencies
  • SQLite — one file, no server to operate
  • Strict validation at the ingestion boundary
  • Separate device and operator credentials
  • Append-only history, current state kept apart

Dashboard

  • Standards-based, no framework and no build step
  • Real moisture history from stored telemetry
  • Explicit offline, stale and empty states
  • Simulated data always labelled as simulated

Impact

What the mechanism does — and what we have not yet measured.

How HYDRAX is designed to reduce waste

  • Water follows the soil, not the calendar. A zone above its start threshold is not irrigated, however long it has been since the last run.
  • Runs end on a measurement. Irrigation stops when the zone reaches its stop threshold rather than when a timer expires.
  • The worst case is bounded. A maximum runtime caps how long a single run can continue if something downstream has failed.
  • Short-cycling is prevented. The hysteresis band, a minimum run time and a per-zone cooldown each independently stop the pump from switching repeatedly.
  • Faults surface as alerts. A stuck run, a failed probe or a silent controller raises an alert instead of continuing quietly.

What we are not claiming

We have not published a water-saving percentage, and we will not until we have measured one. Doing that credibly requires a flow meter, a control plot watered on the existing schedule, and a full season of data. Until those exist, any figure would be marketing rather than evidence.

Nor is pump health scoring, failure prediction or leak localization implemented. The system is architected for them; not one of them is claimed.

About

Why we built it this way.

HYDRAX started from a straightforward observation: a farm's irrigation should not stop making sensible decisions because a router rebooted. That single constraint — the controller must be able to do its job alone — determined most of the architecture that followed.

It is why the control logic contains no networking code, why safety interlocks live in the actuator layer rather than in the decision logic, and why the dashboard is deliberately powerless: it can show you everything and command nothing.

It is also why this site has no performance graph. The engineering is real and can be audited. The field results do not exist yet, and we would rather say so than publish a number we cannot defend.
